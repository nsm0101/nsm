ABP General Pediatrics — Board Prep

A blueprint-weighted study system for the certifying exam on October 14, 2026.

Read this first

The strategy, in four sentences.

The thing that has to change on a retake. If a previous attempt was built on reading, watching, and reviewing, repeating that at higher volume is the most common way to fail again. The single largest evidence-based gain available in nine weeks is switching from recognition (reading, watching, highlighting — all of which feel productive) to retrieval (answering questions cold, then studying only what you missed). A video curriculum such as Hippo is a remediation resource, not the primary activity. Questions come first; content review is what you do in response to a missed question.

Three more principles this plan is built on:

  1. Study proportional to the blueprint, not to your interest. Preventive Pediatrics and Well-Child Care is 12% of the exam — more than cardiology and pulmonology combined. EENT is 6%, more than cardiology alone. Most repeat test-takers over-study subspecialty content and under-study the outpatient bread-and-butter that carries the most questions. The Where the Points Are tab quantifies this.
  2. Space it and interleave it. Every week in this plan revisits earlier material. Blocked practice (all cardiology on Tuesday) feels better and performs worse than interleaved practice on exam day, because the exam does not tell you which domain a question comes from.
  3. Train the conditions, not just the content. Pacing and test anxiety are trainable, but only by rehearsal under real conditions — timed blocks, no pausing, no looking things up mid-block. That starts in Week 3, not the week before the exam.

The error log — the highest-value thing you will build

Keep one running document. For every question you miss, write one line with three parts: (1) the fact you didn't know or misapplied, (2) why you got it wrong, and (3) the one-sentence correction. The "why" is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters — categorize it as one of:

  • Knowledge gap — I did not know this fact. (Content review fixes this.)
  • Misread the stem — I missed the age, the timing, the vaccination status, the word "except." (Technique fixes this.)
  • Answered a different question — I gave the diagnosis when it asked for the next step. (Technique.)
  • Knew it, second-guessed it — I changed a correct answer. (Track this rate. If it's high, the fix is a rule, not more studying.)
  • Rushed — pacing pressure. (Pacing drills.)

By Week 6 the distribution of these five categories tells you exactly where the remaining nine weeks should go. Most repeat test-takers discover that fewer than half their misses are true knowledge gaps — which is good news, because the other categories are faster to fix.

What this table usually reveals

Four observations that catch most candidates by surprise, and are worth checking against your own ratings:

  • Preventive Pediatrics / Well-Child Care is 12% — about 41 questions, the largest domain by a wide margin, and larger than cardiology, pulmonology, and gastroenterology combined (15% together). Milestones, immunization rules, and screening ages are protocolized and completely knowable. If your confidence here is not a 4, this is the cheapest block of points on the exam.
  • EENT is 6% — roughly 20 questions, more than cardiology (5%) and more than neurology (4%). Almost nobody studies otitis media, sinusitis criteria, and the orbital-versus-preseptal distinction proportional to that. This is the most common weighting blind spot.
  • Mental and Behavioral Health is 6% — the blueprint moved it up, and it is now larger than cardiology or GI. Older review books under-cover it. ADHD treatment by age, autism screening, eating disorder admission criteria, and SSRI facts are reliably tested.
  • The bottom eight domains together are only 20% — genetics, rheumatology, ethics, and QI/statistics are 2% each. Do not spend a whole week on inborn errors of metabolism. Ethics and QI/statistics in particular are worth ~7 questions each and are formulaic — a couple of hours on each returns more than a couple of hours on any subspecialty.

How to attack an ABP question

Misreading a stem costs the same point as not knowing the answer, and it is far easier to fix. This is a procedure — run it the same way every time until it's automatic, at which point it costs no extra seconds.

The five-step read

  1. Read the last line first. Before the vignette, read the actual question. "Most likely diagnosis," "most appropriate next step," "most likely causative organism," and "most appropriate initial test" send you through completely different reasoning. Knowing which one you're answering changes what you extract from the stem.
  2. Extract the discriminators as you read. Age is a discriminator in the majority of pediatric questions and is placed in the first six words on purpose. So are: exact duration, immunization status, gestational age and corrected age, travel and animal exposure, medications, family history, and the specific number in a vital sign. If a detail is in the stem, it is there for a reason — ABP items are edited to remove irrelevant content.
  3. Commit to an answer before reading the options. This is the single most protective habit against well-written distractors. If you have an answer in mind and it's in the list, your agreement is meaningful. If you read the options first, plausible distractors will pull you.
  4. Eliminate on a stated reason. For each option you rule out, be able to finish the sentence "this is wrong because…" If you can't, you're eliminating on gut feeling, which is where second-guessing starts.
  5. Answer, flag if unsure, and move. Do not re-derive. Flag it and go — you will have time at the end of the section, and you'll see it with fresher eyes.

The rule on changing answers. The evidence is clear that answer changes are net positive — but only when driven by a specific reason: you re-read the stem and caught a detail you missed, or a later question reminded you of a fact. Changes driven by free-floating anxiety ("this feels too easy") are net negative. Make this an explicit rule for yourself: I change an answer only if I can name the new piece of information that changed my mind. If you can't name it, you leave it. Track how often you break this rule during practice — for people whose failure mode is second-guessing, this rule alone is often worth several questions.

Recurring ABP answer patterns

  • The exam rewards not over-testing. When a stem describes a benign variant in loving detail (erythema toxicum, Still murmur, orthostatic proteinuria, physiologic gynecomastia, umbilical granuloma) and offers four investigations, the answer is reassurance. ABP is written by practicing general pediatricians and consistently rewards clinical restraint.
  • …except when there's a cannot-miss diagnosis, where the answer is aggressive and immediate: absent red reflex, bilious emesis, testicular torsion, SCFE, sentinel bruise in an infant, jaundice in the first 24 hours, direct hyperbilirubinemia, fever in a child with sickle cell disease. Learn which list a finding belongs to and most questions answer themselves.
  • "Next step" is usually simpler than you think. History, physical exam, or a basic bedside test frequently beats advanced imaging. When two answers are both reasonable, choose the one that comes first in a real clinical sequence.
  • Distractors that are true but don't answer the question. A statement can be entirely accurate and still be wrong for the question asked. Check every candidate answer against the actual question stem, not against your general knowledge.
  • Treat, then confirm. When delay causes harm — adrenal crisis, congenital hypothyroidism, cerebral edema in DKA, anaphylaxis, meningitis, torsion — the answer treats first and confirms after. Options that obtain a confirmatory test before treating are the trap.
  • Watch the two-part question. "Which is the most likely diagnosis and the most appropriate treatment?" — both halves must be right. Verify both.
  • Guidelines that changed recently are prime targets. Isotonic maintenance fluids, doxycycline at any age, early peanut introduction, egg allergy and influenza vaccine, rear-facing car seats with no age cutoff, BRUE replacing ALTE, febrile infant risk stratification, universal lipid screening at 9–11. If you learned it more than a few years ago, verify it.

Never leave a question blank

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. On a 340-question exam, blind guessing on 10 unanswered questions returns about 2.5 points; eliminating even one option first roughly doubles that. Before the last five minutes of each section, sweep for unanswered items and fill in every one.

Managing the anxiety, specifically

A retake is harder than a first attempt, not easier — that is a real effect, and it is worth planning for rather than hoping it won't happen.

  • Practice under real conditions, starting Week 3. Anxiety on test day is largely a novelty response. The more closely your practice resembles the real thing — timed, uninterrupted, no lookups, seated at a desk, morning if possible — the less novel test day is. A block you pause to look something up in is not practice; it's studying.
  • Have a written reset protocol for going blank. When it happens mid-section: flag the question, take one slow breath out (longer exhale than inhale), move to the next question, and return later. The goal is to make the response automatic so you're not deciding what to do while panicking. Rehearse it during practice blocks so it's a habit, not a plan.
  • Score-check pressure is the enemy of pacing. Do not try to compute how you're doing mid-exam. You cannot, the estimate will be wrong, and it costs both time and composure. Your only in-section job is the current question and the clock.
  • Expect several questions you have never seen material on. Every candidate gets them, including everyone who passes comfortably. The passing standard accounts for it. Encountering an unfamiliar question is not a signal about how you're doing — treat it as one question, guess intelligently, flag, and move.
  • Between sections, do not review. Use the full break. Eat, drink, move, and go into the next section without re-litigating the last one.

Check the clock at questions 20, 40, and 60 only. Checking more often costs time and feeds anxiety; checking less often lets a deficit grow past recovery. If you're behind at question 40, the correction is to start flagging and moving faster on anything that isn't resolving in 30 seconds — not to speed-read everything.

The long stem trap. A handful of questions have stems two or three times normal length. They are not worth more points. Give one a maximum of about two minutes, then answer with your best read, flag it, and move — those extra minutes come directly out of the questions at the end of the section, which are often easier.

Stamina — the part people don't train

Seven hours of testing is a physical event. Accuracy in section 4 is a trainable skill, and the only way to train it is to do it.

  • Week 7 and Week 8 each include a full-length simulation: four 105-minute blocks with 15-minute breaks, done in one sitting, starting at the same hour your real exam starts. Do not skip these or split them across days — the entire point is the fourth block.
  • Plan your breaks in advance. Breaks are up to 15 minutes each between sections. Decide now what you eat and drink at each one, and rehearse it during the simulations. Protein and moderate carbohydrate; avoid a heavy meal that produces an afternoon crash and avoid so much caffeine that you're jittery by section 3.
  • Move during every break. Stand, walk, stretch. Sitting for seven hours degrades attention measurably.
  • Hydrate on a schedule, not by thirst — but calibrate during the simulations so you know exactly how much you can drink without needing an unscheduled bathroom break, which comes out of your testing time.

The last three days (Oct 12–14)

  • Monday Oct 12: Light review of your error log only. No new material — new material this late produces anxiety, not retention. Confirm the test center location, drive or map the route, and lay out your ID.
  • Tuesday Oct 13: Two hours maximum, all of it your own error log and the trap lists. Stop by early afternoon. Do something unrelated in the evening. Set two alarms.
  • Wednesday Oct 14: Normal breakfast, arrive early, bring your ID and your planned snacks. No studying in the parking lot.
  • Sleep is a performance intervention. Protect the two nights before, not just the last one — the night-before-last has a larger effect on cognition than the night before.

Logistics to confirm well before the week of the exam: your ABP eligibility and registration status, the scheduling permit and your Prometric appointment, the exact test center address and parking, the acceptable form of photo ID and that the name matches your registration exactly, and the center's rules for personal items and food storage. Sort these in September so that October has nothing in it but studying and sleep.
